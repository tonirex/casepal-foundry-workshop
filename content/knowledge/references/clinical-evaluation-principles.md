# Clinical Evaluation — Principles

*Plain-language explainer written for the CasePal workshop. Draws on publicly documented clinical-evaluation principles (IMDRF, EU MDR clinical evaluation guidance, WHO). Not verbatim from any single guideline.*

## What "clinical evaluation" means for a medical device

Clinical evaluation is the process of collecting, appraising, and analysing clinical data on a medical device to demonstrate its safety, performance, and clinical benefit *for the intended use*. The output is a **Clinical Evaluation Report (CER)** — a required dossier component from Class B (measurement) upwards. See SOP-03 for the CER adequacy checklist.

## Three legitimate sources of clinical data

1. **Literature.** A systematic search of published clinical studies on the device (or on equivalent devices). The search strategy, results, and appraisal must be documented — a bare list of references is not a literature review.
2. **Equivalent-device data.** Where the applicant argues clinical equivalence to another device, an **equivalence justification** covering clinical / technical / biological characteristics must be included. Equivalence claims without justification are the most common CER weakness at intake.
3. **Own clinical investigation.** Data generated by the applicant — observational cohort, RCT, or post-market follow-up. Required for Class D devices citing own data.

## Benefit-risk analysis

Every CER culminates in a benefit-risk analysis:

- **Benefits** — clinical effectiveness for the stated indication, quantified where possible.
- **Risks** — from the risk-management file (ISO 14971 aligned), classified by severity and probability.
- **Overall judgement** — do benefits outweigh residual risks for the intended population and use case?

Quantitative benefit-risk analysis is preferred where the underlying data permits; qualitative analysis is acceptable where quantification is not feasible, but the reasoning must be explicit.

## Post-market plan

For devices where residual risk is non-trivial (typically Class C and D), the CER must be linked to:

- A **Post-Market Surveillance (PMS) plan** — passive monitoring: complaints, incident reports, literature scanning.
- A **Post-Market Clinical Follow-up (PMCF) plan** (Class D, some Class C with condition) — active data collection to confirm ongoing clinical performance and detect any drift.

## AI-MD specifics

Because AI-MD performance can drift as clinical practice or data distributions evolve, the CER for an AI-MD **must** address:

- Training-data provenance and representativeness.
- Validation-set characteristics and per-subgroup performance.
- Model-version binding.
- Real-world monitoring for model drift.

See SOP-03 §4 for the query template.

## What CasePal cannot do

- CasePal cannot judge whether a benefit-risk analysis is *correct* — only whether the required *components* are present. Substantive adequacy is the reviewer's judgement.
- CasePal cannot autonomously recommend rejection of a CER — only surface gaps and suggest RFI questions.
