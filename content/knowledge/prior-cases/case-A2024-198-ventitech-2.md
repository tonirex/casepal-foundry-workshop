## Summary

A life-sustaining Class D ventilator intended for adult ICU use.

## Why this was rejected

The dossier had two persistent gaps that could not be closed after two RFI cycles:

1. **Cybersecurity risk assessment absent.** SOP-01 Class D §3.4 requires a cybersecurity risk assessment for connected devices. The applicant supplied only a network-security policy for the manufacturer's corporate infrastructure — not a device-level assessment. Second RFI made the requirement explicit; response continued to conflate corporate and device scope.

2. **Post-market clinical follow-up (PMCF) plan inadequate.** SOP-01 Class D §3.4 requires a PMCF plan. The applicant submitted only a passive incident-reporting form. SOP-03 §3E and the Class D CER guidelines require an **active** post-market monitoring plan for life-sustaining devices — cohort-based follow-up with defined performance endpoints. Second RFI made this explicit; response added a customer-satisfaction survey.

## Additional context

Reviewer noted that the base ventilator technology is well-understood; rejection was purely on the two documentation gaps, not on any safety concern about the device itself. The applicant was informed that a re-submission with an adequate cybersecurity assessment and PMCF plan would be reviewed on its merits.

## Why this precedent is useful

Teaching point: rejection is an **evidence-based outcome after due process**, not a default. Two RFI cycles + specific gap identification + failure to close = rejection. CasePal should surface this precedent whenever a Class D ventilator arrives, particularly to demonstrate the "reject" bar — it is **not** *"they're always incomplete"*, which is exactly the bias Kai brings to Lab 3.
